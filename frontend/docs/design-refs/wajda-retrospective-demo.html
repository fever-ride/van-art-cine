Andrzej Wajda: A Retrospective

Seventeen films screening in Vancouver this winter, from Wajda's earliest work in the 1950s to his final film in 2007.

Andrzej Wajda was one of Poland's most important directors. He led the Polish Film School, the movement that brought Polish cinema to international attention in the mid-1950s. His first three features, A Generation, Kanal, and Ashes and Diamonds, are known as his War Trilogy, among the most studied films of postwar Europe. He kept working for five decades, through communist rule and its collapse. Man of Iron won the Palme d'Or at Cannes in 1981. In 2000, he received an honorary Academy Award for his career. This winter, VIFF Centre in Vancouver presents seventeen of his films, from A Generation in 1955 to Katyn, made in 2007 about the massacre that killed his own father. It is a rare chance to see this much of one director's work on one screen.

1955 – 1965

The Polish Film School

A Generation, Kanal, and Ashes and Diamonds are Wajda's War Trilogy, made as the Polish Film School took shape in the mid-1950s. All three deal with the German occupation of Poland, and together made Wajda internationally known. Innocent Sorcerers is a smaller, contemporary story about a young doctor in Warsaw. The Ashes is an earlier epic, following Polish soldiers under Napoleon.

1969 – 1982

Working Under Censorship

This was Wajda's most prolific period, and the one Polish censors watched most closely. Man of Marble, about a bricklayer turned propaganda hero, was held back by censors for years. Its sequel, Man of Iron, followed the 1980 shipyard strikes that led to Solidarity and won the Palme d'Or at Cannes in 1981. After martial law was declared that year, Wajda had trouble working in Poland and made some films abroad, including Danton, shot in France.

1990 – 2007

Reckoning with History

Wajda's later films turn toward history Poland was not allowed to discuss for decades. Korczak tells the true story of a doctor who stayed with the Jewish orphans in his care rather than leave them in the Warsaw Ghetto. Katyn, made in his eighties, is personal. His father was one of about 22,000 Polish officers killed by Soviet forces at Katyn in 1940. Wajda did not learn the full truth until communism fell.
